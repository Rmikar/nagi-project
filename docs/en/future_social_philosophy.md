# Nagi in Five Minutes

**Nagi is a compass for continuing to build a society where people can live with greater ease.**

Having to obey an unjust demand in order to survive. A role in sustaining society turning into power over others. Nagi asks how we can change these conditions.

Its purpose is to protect human dignity and kindness and enable everyone to take part in culture and creativity. To that end, it proposes priorities for social systems and education.

## Nagi’s two central proposals

1. **Free survival from ownership**
2. **Make the ethics that sustain the world’s cycles the highest priority in education**

Nagi prioritizes non-owned foundations of life in social systems and ethical development in education. It directs funding, people, technology, and time for learning toward these two priorities.

### Proposal 1 | Free survival from ownership

Water, food, energy, health care, shelter, and communication become **Vital Commons**, sustained across communities and generations, rather than assets that someone can own to demand obedience.

Basic access does not depend on income, employment, nationality, reputation, or agreement with an ideology. Costs and maintenance work do not disappear: society must work out how to share them.

Non-Ownership does not mean abandonment. Responsibilities for use, maintenance, renewal, and explanation remain clear, without becoming a right to cut off another person's means of survival. Bodies, private homes, personal belongings, memories, and other private spheres remain protected.

### Proposal 2 | Make the ethics that sustain the world’s cycles the highest priority in education

We learn how natural cycles, people's work, and care sustain our lives. We ask who benefits from our choices, who bears the burdens, and what remains for future generations.

We also study what domination in defense of interests or status has taken from people, and on what scale. Individual experiences and wider social effects help us examine why harmful practices were accepted and difficult to change.

**A role in sustaining people's lives does not confer a right to demand their obedience.** We connect this understanding to decisions in everyday groups and situations in which we hold authority.

Educational resources prioritize history, philosophy, dialogue, and encounters with local production, maintenance, and care. Science and technology are learned alongside questions about their purposes and whose lives they change.

## Why both are necessary

Even without ownership, those allocating resources or deciding on maintenance hold power. If they monopolize knowledge and authority, new domination and entrenched interests can emerge.

Nagi therefore combines institutional limits on power with the cultivation of judgment and shared norms that do not take domination for granted. Practices once accepted can become unacceptable as understanding of their harms spreads.

Secure living conditions make unjust demands easier to refuse. Experience and learning can reinforce norms against holding survival hostage; those norms can help protect basic provision. **Material conditions and ethics are intended to sustain each other.**

Education does not make everyone conform to one model of a good person. Ethics itself remains open to revision in light of its effects, and learners' objections must be able to change educational institutions.

## How everyday life could change

- **Losing a competition would not cost you survival.** Markets, businesses, and competition may remain, without defeat removing the foundations of life.
- **Refusing an unjust order would not cost you your livelihood.** People would have practical ways to leave an organization or community and choose another life.
- **Periods without work would allow rest and return.** Care, relearning, culture, and creativity would be supported as important parts of life.

Nagi does not prescribe the same happiness or way of life for everyone. It proposes a “strong floor” of survival and rights beneath an “open ceiling” of different choices. The extent of that floor and how its burdens are shared still require concrete decisions.

## Keeping these proposals from becoming domination

Three principles support the proposals: **Non-Ownership, Resonance, and Breathing**. They loosen dominating monopolies, allow different voices to be heard without erasing differences, and place rest, recovery, and cycles of renewal at society's center.

Public decisions protect opposition, refusal, non-participation, and exit; silence does not count as consent. Nagi calls this **Resonant Democracy**.

Even well-intentioned institutions can deteriorate. **Institutional Immunity** makes it possible to detect harm, investigate independently, provide remedy, and revise, replace, or end institutions. Oversight bodies are themselves subject to scrutiny and replacement. These are necessary design principles, not guarantees against failure.

AI is optional assistance. It may support equipment, supply networks, discussion, and learning, but does not score human worth or make final decisions on basic provision or rights on its own. Human contact, manual operations, and freedom not to use AI remain available.

## What remains unresolved

Non-Ownership does not create resources. Shortages of food, energy, equipment, or people can make provision difficult. Capacity for production, repair, resource circulation, and reserves must be developed.

Nagi has no finished answers for funding, transition burdens, maintenance staffing, allocation during scarcity, regional inequality, or international cooperation. Whether prioritizing ethical education improves judgment and social sustainability also requires practice and evaluation.

Try changes at a small scale without dismantling existing protections. Evaluate them with affected people; stop and revise them when they cause harm. **Nagi itself remains open to reconsideration as we continue building a society where people can live with greater ease.**
