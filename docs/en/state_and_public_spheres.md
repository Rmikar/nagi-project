# The State Is Not the Goal
## — Survival Does Not Depend on Obedience to a State, and Culture Is Not Owned by Borders

Nagi does not begin by deciding that the state must remain or disappear.

The state currently bundles survival, rights, administration, culture, security, law, redistribution, identity, and broad coordination inside one territorial structure. Nagi unbundles those functions and asks, for each one: At what scale should it operate? Who is affected? Who decides? What can be challenged? What must remain universal? How can authority change or end?

The answer may still include states. It need not treat the state as the permanent owner of every public function.

## 1. Separate survival from membership in a state

Food, water, energy, shelter, health care, communication, and emergency protection do not become rewards for citizenship, nationality, loyalty, military service, tax status, political agreement, or use of a state-approved AI.

Vital Commons are supported locally and across regions. A person who crosses a border, loses documents, refuses an order, or belongs to no recognized state does not therefore lose the floor of life.

This does not erase residence, legal responsibility, or practical limits. It removes the power to turn those categories into a license to stop basic survival.

→ [Vital Commons](vital_commons.html)

## 2. Match the scale of administration to the problem, not automatically to a border

Neighborhood care, a watershed, an energy grid, disease response, migration, language education, an ecosystem, and global climate coordination operate at different scales.

Nagi does not force each problem into the territorial size of one state.

- Decisions remain close to affected people where consequences are local.
- River basins, ecosystems, grids, transport, and public health can use cross-border institutions.
- Rights that protect a person from local domination need wider guarantees and appeal.
- Global risks need broad coordination without creating one unaccountable global center.

Public spheres overlap. Their boundaries remain visible, revisable, and connected by appeal and mutual aid.

## 3. A country may remain as a cultural reference, not a box that owns people

Names, languages, histories, landscapes, food, arts, rituals, and shared memories may continue to matter deeply.

A country can remain an icon through which people refer to culture and history. That icon does not own every person associated with it, define one authentic identity, or make departure a betrayal.

People can inherit, reinterpret, combine, decline, or leave cultural affiliations. Cultural continuity does not assign a life to a role.

## 4. No state represents a culture completely

Culture crosses borders, predates states, changes through encounter, and includes disagreement within every named community.

States may support archives, education, language access, sites, and creators. They do not become the sole author, spokesperson, or owner of culture. Minority, diasporic, Indigenous, local, mixed, and newly created cultures retain their own voices and routes of stewardship.

Opening culture is not permission to extract it. Creator personhood, community context, consent, and the freedom not to share remain protected.

## 5. State functions must be revisable, reducible, and terminable

A public function can acquire value without earning permanent authority.

Terms of office, emergency powers, agencies, databases, borders of jurisdiction, contracts, and exclusive mandates require purposes, limits, review dates, appeal routes, and end conditions.

Success does not entitle an institution to write the next rules in its own favor. Long service does not convert a public role into inherited property.

## 6. Unbundling the state does not mean losing broad solidarity

Local autonomy alone can leave poor regions, minorities, mobile people, and disaster-struck communities without support. Nagi therefore keeps wider redistribution, mutual aid, rights guarantees, technical support, disaster response, and ecological responsibility.

The aim is not isolated localism. It is a federation of overlapping public spheres in which larger scales support what smaller scales cannot do, while larger scales do not absorb every local decision.

## 7. Violence, safety, law, and redistribution remain unresolved if merely renamed

Nagi does not assume that violence disappears when the state is weakened, or that every local community is safe.

Questions remain about protection from organized violence, accountable coercive power, investigation, fair trial, cross-border crime, defense, taxation, redistribution, migration, and the protection of minorities against local majorities.

Any use of force requires strict purpose and scope, independent review, recorded reasons, urgent appeal, protection from discrimination, and an end condition. AI does not autonomously authorize lethal force, detention, punishment, deportation, denial of basic life, or loss of rights.

These are central areas for further legal, historical, institutional, and international research—not details that can be postponed behind a metaphor.

## 8. The state in Nagi remains a revisable instrument

Nagi neither worships the state nor assumes that abolishing it creates freedom.

It asks which functions protect dignity, survival, culture, freedom, and broad cooperation; which have become domination; which should remain local; which require wider guarantees; and which can be changed or ended.

> Preserve what protects life and freedom.  
> Unbundle powers that have been fused together.  
> Do not let any name, border, victory, or institution own the future.

This chapter is not a completed constitutional model. It keeps the state open as a question that must be answered through rights, evidence, historical learning, small trials, Institutional Immunity, and the voices of affected people.
